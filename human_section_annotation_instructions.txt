HUMAN ANNOTATION INSTRUCTIONS
Section-Level Importance Scoring + Top 4 Foundational Papers

Purpose
You are an author of the paper being annotated. Your job is to provide a human judgment of:

1. Section-level importance within your own paper
2. The top 4 cited papers that are most foundational to your work

These instructions are designed to stay as close as possible to the section-level scoring setup used in our LLM pipeline, so that human and LLM annotations are comparable.


==================================================
PART 1: SECTION-LEVEL IMPORTANCE SCORING
==================================================

What to score
Score only sections and subsections.
Do NOT score paragraphs.
Do NOT separately score citations inside sections.

General principle
Treat the paper as a hierarchy.
At each parent node, distribute a fixed budget across its immediate child sections.

You should judge how much ORIGINAL SCIENTIFIC VALUE each child section creates for the paper.

Ask yourself:
"If this section were removed, how much of the paper's scientific contribution would be lost?"

Focus on:
- new methods
- new algorithms
- new theory or proofs
- new formulations
- new experimental design
- new findings or results
- new technical analysis

Give lower scores to sections that mainly:
- motivate the problem
- provide background
- summarize related work
- restate results from other sections
- provide transitions or narrative framing

Important
For section scoring, do NOT give extra credit just because a section cites many papers.
This stage is about the section's intrinsic contribution to the paper's scientific content.


--------------------------------------------------
How to score
--------------------------------------------------

Step 1: Top-level sections
Start with the whole paper budget = 100 points.
Distribute those 100 points across the top-level sections of the paper.

Example:
Introduction: 12
Methodology: 36
Experiments: 34
Related Work: 8
Conclusion: 5
Limitations: 5

The top-level section scores must sum to exactly 100.


Step 2: Subsections
If a top-level section has subsections, then use the score of that parent section as the new fixed budget.
Distribute that parent score across its immediate children.

Example:
If "Experiments" received 34 points overall, and it has 3 subsections, then distribute exactly 34 points across those 3 subsections.

Example:
Experiments total = 34
Datasets: 6
Baselines: 8
Main Results: 20

These subsection scores must sum to exactly the parent score, here 34.


Step 3: Continue recursively if needed
If a subsection itself has child subsections, repeat the same process.
Always distribute only across immediate children.
Always make the children sum exactly to the parent.


--------------------------------------------------
What counts as high importance
--------------------------------------------------

Higher scores:
- the core technical method of the paper
- the main model, algorithm, or framework
- the main theorem/proof/formulation
- the experiments that establish the paper's core claims
- the analysis that is essential to the contribution

Lower scores:
- background or setup
- narrative overview
- broad motivation
- literature review / related work summary
- discussion that mainly repeats earlier claims
- conclusion text that summarizes rather than adds new scientific value


--------------------------------------------------
Calibration guidance
--------------------------------------------------

Use these as rough anchors, not hard rules.

Introduction:
- Usually lower than the main technical and results sections
- Can be moderate if it defines the exact problem framing in a novel way

Related Work / Background:
- Usually low
- Only score higher if it makes a genuinely important analytical distinction that is central to the paper's contribution

Methods / Approach / Framework:
- Usually high
- Often among the most important sections

Experiments / Results / Evaluation:
- Usually high when they provide the evidence for the paper's main claims

Conclusion:
- Usually low to moderate
- It often summarizes rather than creates new value

Limitations:
- Usually low
- Important for transparency, but usually not a primary source of the paper's original scientific value


--------------------------------------------------
What not to do
--------------------------------------------------

Do not score based on:
- writing quality
- how polished the section is
- how long the section is
- how many citations it contains
- how useful it is for helping the reader follow the story

Do score based on:
- how much that section contributes to the paper's actual scientific contribution


--------------------------------------------------
Output format for section scoring
--------------------------------------------------

For each parent section group, write one line per child.
Use section names exactly as provided in the annotation form.

Format:
<section name>: <score>

Example:
Introduction: 12
Methodology: 36
Experiments: 34
Related Work: 8
Conclusion: 5
Limitations: 5

If subsections are provided, annotate them under their parent budget in the same way.


--------------------------------------------------
Quality checks before submission
--------------------------------------------------

Before you submit, verify:

1. At the top level, all section scores sum to exactly 100.
2. For every parent with child sections, the child scores sum exactly to the parent score.
3. You scored scientific contribution, not readability or citation count.
4. You did not score paragraphs or individual citations.


==================================================
PART 2: TOP 4 FOUNDATIONAL PAPERS
==================================================

Goal
Choose the 4 cited papers that are most foundational to your work.

Definition of "foundational"
A foundational paper is one that directly supports the core technical contribution of your paper.

Examples of foundational use:
- a method your work builds on
- a baseline that is central to your evaluation
- a theory or formulation your work depends on
- a prior framework that strongly shaped your design
- a key paper without which your contribution would be substantially different

Examples of non-foundational use:
- broad background references
- generic motivation references
- citations included mainly for completeness
- peripheral comparisons
- papers mentioned only in passing


--------------------------------------------------
How to choose the 4 papers
--------------------------------------------------

Pick exactly 4 cited papers.
Rank them from 1 to 4, where:
- Rank 1 = most foundational
- Rank 4 = still foundational, but less central than 1-3

Please use the citation identifiers exactly as given in the annotation form.
If full titles are provided, include the title too.


--------------------------------------------------
Output format for foundational papers
--------------------------------------------------

Format:
1. <citation id or citation string> — <short reason>
2. <citation id or citation string> — <short reason>
3. <citation id or citation string> — <short reason>
4. <citation id or citation string> — <short reason>

Reason length:
1 short sentence per paper is enough.

Example:
1. (Smith et al., 2021) — This is the main technical method we build on.
2. (Lee and Chen, 2020) — This baseline is central to our empirical evaluation.
3. (Wang et al., 2019) — Our formulation directly follows this setup.
4. (Garcia et al., 2022) — This paper shaped the framework we extend.
